# Transitions and Motion Direction

Use motion to clarify story, guide attention, and preserve continuity—not as decoration on every cut.

## 1. Choose the transition by editorial purpose

- **Hard cut:** default for clear action changes, punchy pacing, and most dialogue edits.
- **Match cut:** connect shots through shape, movement, framing, color, or action.
- **Cross-dissolve:** signal time passing, memory, softness, or a deliberate blend. Avoid using it to hide continuity errors.
- **Fade to/from black:** mark a meaningful opening, ending, or chapter break.
- **Wipe / directional reveal:** use only when the direction has narrative or brand meaning.
- **Whip pan / motion blur transition:** use sparingly; both shots need compatible movement and direction.
- **Graphic transition:** reserve for a designed visual system, not as a default effect.

Document each transition with its editorial reason, duration, direction, and any required source/target handles.

## 2. Motion hierarchy

1. Subject/action motion carries the story.
2. Camera motion establishes perspective or emphasis.
3. Graphic motion supports information hierarchy.
4. Decorative motion is optional and should never compete with the message.

For each shot, specify camera behavior precisely: locked-off, pan, tilt, dolly, push-in, pull-back, orbit, crane, handheld, or tracking. State the subject's movement separately from camera movement.

## 3. Timing and easing

- Set timing in seconds and convert to frames using the project's FPS.
- Keep important actions readable; do not accelerate gestures merely to fit narration.
- Use linear motion for mechanical movement and constant-speed travel.
- Use ease-in/ease-out for natural starts and stops.
- Use spring/overshoot only when the visual language supports it.
- Avoid excessive bounce, elastic motion, and simultaneous competing movements.
- Keep transitions within available clip handles; do not create unintended gaps or overlaps.

## 4. Continuity checklist

Track screen direction, eyelines, subject position, prop placement, hand occupancy, lighting direction, focal length feel, and state of food/products/objects. A later shot must begin from the actual end state of the previous shot when continuity is required.

## 5. Accessibility and restraint

Avoid rapid flashing, high-frequency strobing, and motion that causes unnecessary discomfort. Respect reduced-motion requirements when the delivery context supports them. Do not animate essential information in a way that makes it unreadable.

## 6. Timeline representation

Represent transitions explicitly in the project timeline. Do not infer a transition from adjacent clip boundaries. Validate that transition duration fits both clips and that the intended overlap is supported by the chosen render engine. Engine-specific syntax belongs in an adapter or implementation note, not in the universal project schema.

## 7. Final review

Watch the rendered transition at normal speed and frame-by-frame around its boundaries. Check for black gaps, flashes, frozen frames, abrupt scale changes, mismatched direction, and audio clicks. A structurally valid timeline does not prove that a transition looks correct.
